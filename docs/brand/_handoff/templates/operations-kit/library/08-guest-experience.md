# 2 · SANDBAR & SWIM CALL POLICY

## 2.1 Swim calls are not guaranteed

A swim call happens only when conditions allow. It may be cancelled for current, wind, chop, marine traffic, water quality, wildlife, visibility, time, or the Master's judgment. **A cancelled swim call is not grounds for a refund** — see the Weather Policy. Please don't book expecting one on a specific day.

## 2.2 The rules

Swimming is permitted **only** when: the Master has announced an authorized swim call · **engines are shut down** · the swim ladder is deployed · the swim flag is displayed · a crew member is on watch.

- **Head count in, head count out.** Answer when your name or number is called.
- Stay within the marked area and in sight of the crew.
- **No diving. No jumping from the upper deck, the bow, the roof, or anywhere else.** Depth at the sandbar runs as little as one to four feet depending on tide, and a dive into three feet of water is a spinal injury.
- Non-swimmers wear a PFD in the water. Tell the crew — nobody will comment.
- Minors enter only with their accompanying adult **in the water with them.**
- Return immediately on the recall signal.
- No swimming under or around the vessel, near the engines, or between rafted boats.
- No alcohol in the water.

## 2.3 Conditions to understand

**Current.** Tidal flow near Haulover Inlet is strong and gets stronger as the tide runs. Water that looks flat can move you a long way very quickly. Stay upstream of the vessel where possible and don't rely on being able to swim back.

**Depth.** The sandbar shifts constantly. Depth varies from roughly one to four feet across the flats and drops off sharply near the marked channel. Never assume the bottom is where it was last time.

**Traffic.** Sandbars are busy. Vessels transit close, and wake and propellers are the primary hazard. Stay in the marked area and stay visible.

**Water quality.** We check advisories before every swim call. Swim calls are cancelled for water quality advisories, algal bloom, or post-rainfall runoff conditions.

**Marine life.** Jellyfish, sea lice, and stingrays are seasonally present. **Shuffle your feet** rather than stepping down — it moves rays out of the way instead of onto your foot.

## 2.4 Anchoring and where we go

The Master selects the anchorage. Note that Biscayne Bay contains designated **Anchoring Limitation Areas** — including waters around Star Island, Hibiscus Island, and Watson Island — and anchoring is prohibited within `[150]` feet of marinas and boat ramps and `[500]` feet of superyacht facilities. We anchor on sand and avoid seagrass. Where we drop is a legal and environmental decision, not a preference.

## 2.5 Rafting up

Where the Company rafts to other vessels, it is at the Master's discretion only. **Guests may not board another vessel, and guests from other vessels may not board ours** — our manifest, our capacity, our COI, our liability.

## 2.6 Water toys

Club-issued floats and equipment only. Personal inflatables, towables, and large floats are prohibited — they drift, they obscure swimmers from other vessels, and they become marine debris. `[Adjust if you issue and control specific equipment.]`

## 2.7 Leaving

The recall signal is `[SIGNAL]`. Come in promptly. **The vessel does not get underway until the head count reconciles**, so a slow swimmer delays everyone and, more importantly, a missing count triggers an emergency response.

# 3 · MUSIC, SOUND & NOISE POLICY

## 3.1 Sound is programmed, not democratic

Audio aboard is curated and controlled from the helm. This is partly a taste decision and entirely a safety one: **the Master must be able to cut all sound instantly to make an announcement.** A guest speaker that can't be muted from the helm is a genuine hazard in an emergency.

Personal speakers, portable sound systems, and Bluetooth devices connecting to the vessel's system are prohibited. See Prohibited Items.

## 3.2 The legal frame

**Miami-Dade County's noise ordinance restricts music audible more than 100 feet from a vessel between 11:00 PM and 7:00 AM.** Broward County and its municipalities maintain comparable restrictions. Levels are managed to comply, and volume drops when we're near residences, moored vessels, anchorages, and protected areas.

Enforcement in this market is real and unforgiving. A noise citation puts the operation's dock privileges and its relationship with FWC and marine patrol at risk — which is a bigger cost than any single sailing is worth.

## 3.3 How levels are managed

| Situation | Level |
|---|---|
| Open water, daytime | Programmed level |
| Near residences, marinas, moored vessels | Reduced |
| At anchor near other boats | Reduced — sound carries astonishingly far over water |
| After `[11:00 PM]` | Reduced to comply with the ordinance |
| Safety announcement | **Cut to zero, immediately** |
| Docking and maneuvering | Off or minimal |

## 3.4 Voices carry too

Conversation and shouting travel much further over water than people expect — a normal voice at anchor is clearly audible several hundred feet away. At anchorages and near residences, keep it down. This is the part of the policy most often forgotten and most often the actual source of a complaint.

## 3.5 Requests and talent

`[Describe: whether requests are taken, how talent is programmed, and who controls the booth.]` Guests may not access the DJ booth, mixer, or sound equipment.

## 3.6 Complaints

If a nearby vessel or resident raises a concern, we lower the volume immediately and apologize. We do not argue about whether we were within the ordinance. Being right is worth less than being welcome.

# 4 · ENVIRONMENTAL PLEDGE

**We operate in a bay that is in trouble, and we'd like to still be sailing on it in twenty years.**

## 4.1 Nothing goes over the side

No trash, cans, bottles, cigarette butts, food, ice, fruit, or plastic. Federal law (MARPOL Annex V) and Florida law both prohibit it, and it is a Zero Tolerance violation.

Everything you bring aboard leaves with you or goes in a receptacle. **We operate pack-in, pack-out.**

## 4.2 Confetti, glitter, and balloons

Prohibited outright, including "biodegradable" varieties, which do not meaningfully biodegrade in salt water. They are eaten by turtles and seabirds. There is no version of these that we allow.

## 4.3 Sewage

The vessel's head discharges to a **holding tank**, pumped out dockside at `[FACILITY]`. Nothing is discharged in the bay, ever. Guests must never operate any valve aboard.

## 4.4 Seagrass and anchoring

We anchor on **sand only.** Seagrass beds are nursery habitat for most of what lives in this bay, and a prop scar takes decades to recover. The Master will move the vessel rather than anchor on grass, even if it means a longer tender or a less convenient spot.

## 4.5 Manatees and wildlife

- We observe all **manatee protection speed zones** and idle-speed and no-wake zones. Expect the vessel to move slowly through them.
- **Do not feed, touch, chase, pursue, or swim toward marine wildlife.** Manatees and dolphins are federally protected under the Marine Mammal Protection Act, and harassment carries substantial penalties.
- If wildlife approaches the vessel, we shut down and let it pass.
- Report any injured or entangled animal to the crew.

## 4.6 Sunscreen

We ask for **lotion sunscreen, reef-conscious where you can.** Aerosols are prohibited outright — they're a fire risk, they make the deck slick, and most of the spray lands in the water rather than on you.

## 4.7 Fuel and oil

No fuel or oil discharge, ever. Absorbent materials aboard. Any sheen is reported immediately to the National Response Center and the Coast Guard.

## 4.8 What we ask of you

Bring a reusable bottle · use the receptacles · pick up something that isn't yours if you see it · shuffle your feet in the shallows so you don't step on a ray · and take the photo without touching the animal.

# 6 · SUN & HEAT SAFETY GUIDE

**The most common medical incident on this vessel is not a fall or a marine injury. It is heat, and it is almost entirely preventable.**

## 6.1 Why it's worse out here

Open deck, no shade to walk into, an aluminum surface that radiates heat upward, and water reflecting UV back at you from below. Add wind — which removes the sensation of heat without reducing the exposure — and people burn and dehydrate without feeling it happen. Sea breeze is the most effective sunburn delivery mechanism ever devised.

## 6.2 Before you sail

Hydrate the day before, not just the morning of · eat something · lotion sunscreen SPF `[30]`+ applied 20 minutes before boarding · hat and sunglasses · a light long-sleeve layer, which beats sunscreen for extended exposure.

## 6.3 Aboard

- **Drink water continuously.** Roughly one bottle per hour in summer, more if you're drinking alcohol. Don't wait to feel thirsty — thirst is a late signal.
- Reapply sunscreen every **90 minutes**, and immediately after swimming.
- **Alternate alcohol with water, one for one.** This single habit prevents most heat incidents we see.
- Use the shaded areas. Rotate through them; don't let one group hold them all day.
- Rinse with fresh water after swimming — salt on skin accelerates burning.

## 6.4 Warning signs

**Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, cool clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, muscle cramps, fast weak pulse.
**→ Tell a crew member. Move to shade. Sip water. Cool with wet towels. Lie down with feet elevated.**

**Heat stroke — a medical emergency:** body temperature above 103°F, hot **dry** skin or sweating that has stopped, confusion, slurred speech, agitation, loss of consciousness.
**→ Tell the crew immediately. This kills people. Do not wait to see if it improves and do not give fluids to someone who is confused or unconscious.**

**Dehydration:** dark urine, dry mouth, headache, fatigue, dizziness, reduced urination.

## 6.5 Watch out for each other

The person experiencing heat illness is frequently the last to recognize it — confusion is a symptom, which means they cannot reliably assess themselves. **If someone in your group is acting off, seems confused, has stopped sweating, or is unusually quiet, tell a crew member.** You will not be wasting anyone's time.

## 6.6 Higher risk

Guests over 60 or under 12 · pregnancy · cardiovascular conditions · diabetes · certain medications, including diuretics, beta blockers, antihistamines, and some antidepressants · anyone drinking alcohol · anyone who arrived dehydrated or hungover.

## 6.7 What we provide

`[Shaded seating · free cold water throughout · fresh water rinse · ice · cooling towels · air-conditioned space if applicable]`. Water is free and always available. Ask any crew member — you never need a reason.

# 7 · SEASICKNESS, PREGNANCY & MEDICAL ADVISORY

**Read this before you book, not after you board.**

## 7.1 The honest frame

This vessel operates on open water, sometimes an hour or more from a dock. **Emergency medical response is delayed by definition** — a shoreside ambulance arrives in minutes; offshore, response is measured in tens of minutes at best, and depends on conditions, position, and the availability of Coast Guard or fire rescue marine units. Please factor that into your decision honestly rather than optimistically.

## 7.2 Seasickness

**Prevention works. Treatment mostly doesn't.** Take medication `[30–60]` minutes before boarding — once symptoms start, oral medication rarely catches up.

Options: over-the-counter antihistamines `[dimenhydrinate, meclizine]` · scopolamine patch, by prescription, applied hours ahead · acupressure bands · ginger, which some people find helps.

Aboard: sit low and centered, near the middle of the vessel · **look at the horizon, not your phone** · get fresh air · eat something light · avoid reading and screens · avoid excess alcohol · tell the crew early rather than toughing it out.

If you're prone to it, consult your doctor or pharmacist, and be aware that these medications cause drowsiness and interact poorly with alcohol.

## 7.3 Pregnancy

`[SET POLICY — a common charter standard is no sailing after 24 weeks. Confirm with counsel and your insurer.]`

Considerations to be honest about: vessel motion and impact loading, which can be sharp in chop · heat and dehydration risk, which is elevated in pregnancy · limited medical access offshore · boarding across a moving gangway · restricted medication options for motion sickness.

We recommend discussing it with your physician. Please tell us at booking so crew are aware and seating can be arranged in the most stable part of the vessel.

## 7.4 Conditions to disclose at booking

Cardiac conditions · epilepsy or seizure disorder · diabetes · severe allergies, especially anaphylaxis · asthma or respiratory conditions · mobility limitations · recent surgery · blood clotting disorders or anticoagulant therapy · any condition requiring emergency medication · **non-swimmer status.**

Disclosure is confidential, held per the Privacy Policy, and used only for safety and accommodation. It is never used for pricing or membership decisions.

## 7.5 Bring your own emergency medication

**If you carry an epinephrine auto-injector, rescue inhaler, insulin, or seizure rescue medication, bring it — and tell a crew member where it is at check-in.** We carry a first aid kit, AED, and oxygen, but we do not carry prescription medication and cannot administer what we don't have.

## 7.6 Please reconsider sailing if

You are acutely ill or feverish · contagious · recovering from recent surgery without medical clearance · experiencing an unstable cardiac or respiratory condition · unable to board or move about unassisted where no accommodation has been arranged · under the influence of alcohol or a substance at boarding · experiencing symptoms you would ordinarily see a doctor about.

Contact `[CLUB EMAIL]` — we would far rather move you to another sailing than have you spend the day miles from a hospital wishing you hadn't come.

## 7.7 Non-swimmers

**Tell us. Nobody comments, nobody makes it a thing.** Being a non-swimmer is completely fine and extremely common — knowing about it changes how we watch the deck and how we respond if something happens, which is worth far more than whatever mild awkwardness you're anticipating.

## 7.8 Aboard

Tell any crew member if you feel unwell. Early is better. See the Emergency Procedures.
